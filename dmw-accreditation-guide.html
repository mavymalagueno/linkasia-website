DMW Accreditation: What Foreign Employers Should Know

Before any Filipino worker can be legally deployed to your company, your company must be accredited by the Philippine Department of Migrant Workers. Here is what that means, why it exists, and what it protects you from.

DMW ComplianceAccreditationGlobal Hiring

Executive summary

  • Accreditation is a legal prerequisite, not a formality — deployment without it is prohibited under Philippine law.
  • It verifies three things: that your business is real, the job offer is real, and the contract meets Philippine minimum standards.
  • A licensed agency carries you through it. Link Asia handles the accreditation process for employers as part of the engagement, including first-timers.

Why the Philippines accredits employers

The Philippines created the Department of Migrant Workers under Republic Act No. 11641, consolidating the overseas employment functions previously spread across several agencies, including the former POEA. One of the department's core jobs is making sure that every foreign employer receiving Filipino workers is legitimate and that every contract meets minimum standards.

Accreditation is how that is done. Before deployment, the employer — or their authorised representative — is vetted: company registration documents, a formal manpower request, and the employment contract itself are submitted for verification. Only after the contract passes verification can recruitment proceed to deployment.

What accreditation protects you from

It is tempting to read government requirements as friction. In practice, accreditation protects employers in three concrete ways. It prevents you from unknowingly building a hiring pipeline that is illegal on the Philippine side — which can end in workers being stopped at the airport. It forces contract clarity upfront, which is where most employment disputes are prevented. And it puts a licensed Philippine agency legally on the hook for the recruitment conducted in your name.

What you will be asked to provide

Requirements vary by destination country and worker category, but employers should expect to provide company registration papers (commercial registration or the local equivalent), a manpower request describing the positions and headcount, and the employment contract for verification. Where a Migrant Workers Office covers your country, verification typically runs through it.
